Information Design Prototypes

My first area of exploration is related to visual feedback in the musical garden. Since the interface is designed for young children with limited language skills, I want the interactions to communicate clearly without relying on written instructions. Visual feedback can help children recognise the connection between their actions and the resulting musical response as they interact with different garden elements.

I am concerned with several aspects of the feedback design. Firstly, noticeability — whether the visual response is clear enough for young children to recognise that their action has caused something to happen. Secondly, cause and effect — whether the feedback supports a clear connection between pressing a garden element, seeing a visual response, and hearing a sound. I am also interested in playfulness, as the feedback should encourage curiosity and repeated exploration without becoming distracting or visually overwhelming. Since similar interactions may eventually be applied across multiple garden elements, I also want to consider how consistently the feedback can communicate interactivity throughout the wider interface.

To explore these possibilities, I developed three parallel prototypes using scale feedback, glow feedback, and musical note feedback. The main sound interaction remains consistent across the prototypes, while the form of visual feedback changes. This allows each prototype to explore a different way of communicating interaction while remaining connected to the project's values of Playfulness, Curiosity, and Accessibility.

My second area of exploration is Spatial Input and how movement can be used to control sound in the musical garden. Instead of using sliders, knobs, or other technical music controls, I wanted to explore a more direct way for young children to change sound. In these prototypes, the user can press and drag a garden object, and the movement of the object changes the sound at the same time.

I focused on a few things when exploring this interaction. First was directness — whether children could understand that moving the object was causing the sound to change. Second was mapping — whether the direction of movement and the change in sound had a clear relationship. I also considered how easy the interaction would be for young children, because clicking, holding, and dragging requires more coordination than simply pressing a button. Because of this, I wanted to explore both simple and more complex ways of using movement rather than assuming that more control would always be better.

To explore this area, I developed three parallel prototypes with different Spatial Input mappings. The first prototype uses horizontal dragging to control pitch, the second uses vertical dragging to control volume, and the third combines both, with horizontal movement controlling pitch and vertical movement controlling volume. The dragging interaction stays the same across the three prototypes, while the way movement affects the sound changes. This allows me to explore which kinds of spatial interaction could support Playfulness, Curiosity, and Accessibility within the musical garden.
