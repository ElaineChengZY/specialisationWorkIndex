Information Design Prototypes

My project explores a browser-based musical garden for young children with limited language skills. The interaction is designed to support musical exploration without requiring written instructions or technical music controls. For my parallel prototypes, I explored two design areas: visual feedback and Spatial Input. These areas are connected to the project values of Playfulness, Curiosity, and Accessibility.

Design Area 1: Visual Feedback

My first area of exploration is visual feedback in the musical garden. Since the interface is designed for young children with limited language skills, the interaction needs to communicate clearly without relying on written instructions. Visual feedback can help children recognise that their action has caused a response and connect what they see with what they hear.

I am concerned with several aspects of the feedback design. Firstly, noticeability — whether the visual response is clear enough for young children to recognise that something has happened. Secondly, cause and effect — whether pressing a garden element creates an immediate and understandable connection between the action, the visual response, and the sound. I am also interested in playfulness, because the feedback should encourage curiosity and repeated exploration without becoming distracting or visually overwhelming.

To explore these possibilities, I developed three parallel prototypes using scale feedback, glow feedback, and musical note feedback. The basic sound interaction remains consistent across the prototypes while the form of visual feedback changes. This allows each prototype to investigate a different way of communicating interaction while keeping the main interaction consistent.

Prototype 1: Scale Feedback

This prototype explores scale as a form of immediate visual feedback. When the garden object is pressed, it becomes larger while producing sound. The change in size creates a visible response directly on the object being interacted with, which may help young children connect their action with the resulting sound. Scale feedback is worth exploring because it does not introduce an additional visual element to the interface. Instead, the garden object itself responds to the child's action. This keeps the feedback closely connected to the interaction and may support a simple cause-and-effect relationship without requiring written explanation.
